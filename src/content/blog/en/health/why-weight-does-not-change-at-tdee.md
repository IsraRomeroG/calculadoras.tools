If you eat near your true TDEE, a stable weight is the expected long-term result. If you expected to lose or gain, the issue may be the goal—not a broken metabolism.

TDEE is an estimate. Activity factors can overstate steps, physical work or training, and food portions can differ from labels or tracking entries. Everyday movement may also fall when intake decreases, partly offsetting a planned deficit.

Do not judge the result from a few weigh-ins. Water, sodium, carbohydrate storage, digestion, training soreness and the menstrual cycle can move scale weight independently of fat. Weigh under similar conditions and compare weekly averages for at least two or three weeks.

If the average remains stable, your current intake is probably close to maintenance even if the calculator predicted otherwise. Update your [TDEE estimate](/en/health/tdee-calculator), check assumptions and make one small change. Avoid repeatedly cutting calories in response to normal fluctuations.

Seek professional advice if weight changes unexpectedly, symptoms are present or intake is already highly restricted. A calculator is a starting model, not a measurement of your metabolism.
